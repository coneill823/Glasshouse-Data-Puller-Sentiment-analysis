      How this works, and what it cannot tell you

      Actions, not words

      Everything here is built from what people did in office, weighted by how directly the act changes anything:

      • Votes that use the powers of Parliament — passing a stage of a bill, amending it, approving an order — decide your House of Commons match. They are the only votes that quiz counts.
      • Motions that only express a view (“this House regrets…”) are left out of the House of Commons match, however heated the debate. The devolved legislatures are different, for a reason given below.
      • Motions signed are shown as activity. Signing one is entirely the member's own choice, but it changes no law, so it never feeds a score.
      • Questions and speeches tell you what a member spends time on, never which side they are on. Words are too easy to misread, so they are counted and not interpreted.

      Nothing comes from news coverage, social media, donations or anyone's opinion of a person, including ours.

      Whose judgement is in the House of Commons quiz

      Deciding that a vote was “for” or “against” something is a judgement, and it is not ours. The 36 statements about this parliament and the 95 about earlier ones, the votes behind each one and which way each vote counts are the published policy groupings of TheyWorkForYou, run by the non-partisan charity mySociety. Every score on this site can be traced to the named divisions behind it — open any statement in your results to see them.

      A member's position on a statement is simply their votes on it: votes for, minus votes against, over the votes they cast. Absences count for nothing either way, and decisions made without a recorded vote are excluded, because nobody individually did anything. Where the same policy was voted on in earlier parliaments those votes count too, so a long-serving MP's position rests on more votes than a new one's; each match shows how many.

      The Scottish Parliament, the Senedd and the Assembly

      Nobody publishes policy groupings for the devolved legislatures, and we will not invent them. So their questions involve no grouping at all: each one is a single vote the legislature actually held, and you say how you would have voted. There are questions for each.

      Which votes are asked is fixed by a rule rather than chosen by subject: the most recent votes of the current term on a whole bill or a whole motion — never an amendment, a single clause or a point of procedure — where at least one member in ten of those voting was on the losing side, one question per bill or motion, up to 30. Where a new term has fewer than 20 such votes, the newest from the term before fill the gap.

      The wording is the legislature's own title, and the motion as it was put where its records give it. Voting aye is “for”; nothing is paraphrased.

      • Most of these are motions that express a view, not laws. The devolved legislatures divide on few bills, so leaving such motions out, as the Commons quiz does, would leave almost nothing to ask. Voting on one is still something each member did, but it changes less than a vote on a bill. Every question is marked Bill or Motion so you can tell.
      • One vote is thinner evidence than a Commons statement, which pools several. Members sometimes vote against a motion they sympathise with because of its wording or who proposed it.
      • The Scottish Parliament and the Senedd were elected in May 2026, so most of their questions come from the term before. A member first elected in 2026 did not vote on those, and gets a match only once they have voted on enough.
      • The Senedd's record gives each vote's title but not its wording. Follow the link on a question before answering if its title could be read either way.
      • Figures from different parliaments cannot be compared with each other: they are answers to different questions.

      When a match is shown

      A figure is shown once you and a member both have a view on at least five things, and it always says how many it rests on.

      To be ranked among those who voted most like you, or counted in a party's average, a member must also have voted on at least half of what you answered, out of the votes held while they were a member. Someone elected in 2024 could not have voted on tuition fees in 2004, and that is not held against them. Someone who was a member and did not vote has left a gap. A few sitting MPs have voted on under half of this parliament's statements: the Speaker and deputies, who do not vote, and some who take part in few votes. You still see their figure and what it rests on, marked as a partial picture, but it is not ranked above members with a full record.

      What a vote does and does not mean

      A vote is not an opinion. Most votes are whipped: the typical member votes with their party about 99% of the time, so on most statements your MP's record is their party's. And members vote against things for different reasons — an opposition MP may oppose a bill because it goes too far, or not far enough. What the record does show, without interpretation, is how each of them used the power you gave them.

      The questions follow what was actually voted on, which is mostly the government's programme. Subjects no parliament has voted on are not here.

      The compass

      The compass is the one interpretive layer we add, and it covers the House of Commons only. Each statement that sits clearly on an economic (left–right) or social (liberal–authoritarian) axis is tagged with one: 66 are, and 65 that do not are left untagged. You and every MP are placed with the same tags, so any mistake in them moves everyone identically.

      As a check, the tags were compared with the structure of the voting record itself, with no tagging at all. Among sitting MPs on this parliament's statements, the leading pattern lines up with our economic axis (correlation 0.98) and the second with our social axis (0.76). Across every statement and everyone who has voted on them since 1997 the economic axis still holds (0.89) but the social one is much weaker (0.40): over thirty years, what divides MPs on social questions has changed more than our one axis can follow. Treat the up-and-down position as rougher than the left-and-right one. It shows where votes sit, not what anyone believes.

      The record

      Turnout is the share of divisions a member voted in this term, out of those they were eligible for. Missing a vote is not always a choice: ministers are often excused, members are “paired” with an opponent so both can be absent, and illness or parental leave leaves a gap. Broke with party counts divisions where a member did something different from most of their party at the time — each one is listed. Works on counts which subjects their questions and speeches mention, sorted with keyword lists; that is categorisation rather than opinion, but it is the one place a list we wrote touches these numbers.

      People who do not represent you now

      Searching by name also finds 1,479 former MPs, MSPs and MSs and the 815 members of the House of Lords. They are shown the same way, by what they did with their votes, and a former MP can be matched with your answers on the statements they voted on. Peers are not elected, and there is no quiz for the Lords.

      Candidates in an election that has not happened yet are listed under the place they are standing. The list is Democracy Club's, compiled by volunteers from each council's official notices. A candidate has done nothing in office, so none is given a score; one who has held a seat before links to that record. Only a name, a party and where they are standing are kept. Nobody appears until they are on that register: this site does not guess who might stand.

      Councillors are a trial, with two councils so far. What it found is that councils do not record who voted which way — most votes are a show of hands, written down only as carried or lost — so a councillor cannot be matched with your views from their record. Where a council publishes attendance, that is shown, with what it covers, and nothing more is made of it.

      What is missing

      • Former members of the Northern Ireland Assembly, and votes there before the current term.
      • Mayors, police and crime commissioners and the London Assembly, except as candidates when an election is coming.
      • A Welsh postcode finds your MP but not yet your Senedd constituency: search for that by name. Northern Ireland Assembly seats still use pre-2024 boundaries, so near an edge the lookup can return the neighbouring seat.
      • Postcode lookup needs this page to be hosted on the web; opened as a file, search by name instead.

      What this site knows about you

      • Your answers never leave this browser. They are political opinions, so they are kept in this browser's own storage and nowhere else. Clearing your answers, or this site's data, removes them.
      • A postcode you search for is sent to postcodes.io, an open lookup service, to find its constituencies, ward and council. Elections and councillors are then found in this page from those. Searching by name sends nothing.
      • The typefaces are loaded from Google Fonts. That request carries nothing about you or your answers beyond what any web page request does.
      • There are no accounts, no adverts and no analytics.
