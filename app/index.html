    How this works, and what it cannot tell you

    Actions, not words

    Everything here is built from what representatives did, weighted by how directly the act changes anything:

    • Votes that use the powers of Parliament — passing a stage of a bill, amending it, approving an order — decide your match. These are the only votes the quiz counts.
    • Motions that only express a view (“this House regrets…”) are left out of the match, however heated the debate.
    • Motions signed are shown as activity. Signing one is entirely the member's own choice, but it changes no law, so it never feeds a score.
    • Questions and speeches tell you what a member spends time on, never which side they are on. Words are too easy to misread, so they are counted and not interpreted.

    Whose judgement is in the quiz

    Deciding that a vote was “for” or “against” something is a judgement, and it is not ours. The 36 statements, the votes behind each one and which way each vote counts are the published policy groupings of TheyWorkForYou, run by the non-partisan charity mySociety. Every score on this site can be traced to the named divisions behind it — open any statement in your results to see them.

    A member's position on a statement is simply their votes on it: votes for, minus votes against, over the votes they cast. Absences count for nothing either way, and decisions made without a recorded vote are excluded, because nobody individually did anything. Where the same policy was voted on in earlier parliaments those votes count too, so a long-serving MP's position rests on more votes than a new one's; each match shows how many.

    What a vote does and does not mean

    A vote is not an opinion. Most votes are whipped: the typical member votes with their party about 99% of the time, so on most statements your MP's record is their party's. And MPs vote against things for different reasons — an opposition MP may oppose a bill because it goes too far, or not far enough. What the record does show, without interpretation, is how each MP used the power you gave them.

    Because the statements must have been voted on in this parliament, they mostly follow the government's programme. Subjects Parliament has not voted on are not here.

    The compass

    The compass is the one interpretive layer we add. Each statement that sits clearly on an economic (left–right) or social (liberal–authoritarian) axis is tagged with one; thirteen that do not are left untagged. You and every MP are placed with the same tags, so any mistake in them moves everyone identically. As a check, the tags were compared with the structure of the voting record itself: with no tagging at all, the leading pattern in MPs' positions lines up with our economic axis (correlation 0.98) and the second with our social axis (0.76). It shows where votes sit, not what anyone believes.

    The record

    Turnout is the share of divisions a member voted in this term, out of those they were eligible for. Missing a vote is not always a choice: ministers are often excused, members are “paired” with an opponent so both can be absent, and illness or parental leave leaves a gap. Broke with party counts divisions where a member did something different from most of their party at the time — each one is listed. Works on counts which subjects their questions and speeches mention, sorted with keyword lists; that is categorisation rather than opinion, but it is the one place a list we wrote touches these numbers.

    What is missing

    • The quiz covers the House of Commons only. Nobody publishes an independent vote-by-vote policy mapping for the Scottish Parliament, the Senedd or the Northern Ireland Assembly, and we will not invent one quietly. Their members' records are shown without a match.
    • Postcode lookup uses the open postcodes.io service and needs this page to be hosted on the web. Northern Ireland Assembly seats still use pre-2024 boundaries, so near an edge the lookup can return the neighbouring seat.
